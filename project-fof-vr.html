Project Origin

This project began from observing my grandmother. After falling down the stairs in her fifties, her leg strength and walking confidence gradually declined. She used to enjoy walking outdoors; over time she became less willing to go out.

“Fear was not only about falling again — it gradually changed the willingness to move.”

When I later encountered the concept of Fear of Falling, I recognised this as a self-reinforcing loop between anxiety, avoidance, reduced movement and declining confidence. This experience led me to ask: how can embodied spatial interaction be used to investigate task-level confidence, action, and perceived safety?

Problem

Fear of Falling as a Self-Reinforcing Loop

Concerns about falling can be associated with reduced mobility, confidence, and social participation for some older adults — a conceptual loop of avoidance and decline. When movement is avoided, the loop can reinforce itself.

The research question of this project: Can a VR prototype be used to explore relationships among spatial challenge, action, and task-level confidence in older adults with Fear of Falling?

Research Insight

How might embodied spatial interaction in VR relate to task-level confidence?

Key Evidence

Evidence 01

The avoidance loop can be self-reinforcing

Avoidance may contribute to reduced activity and deconditioning over time, which can in turn reinforce fear for some older adults.

Source: Fear-avoidance literature

Evidence 02

Confidence may relate to action

Task confidence may relate to how a person approaches everyday movement, independent of actual physical ability.

Source: Self-efficacy research

Evidence 03

Graded spatial challenges can be presented in VR

Spatial challenge variations can be presented within a controlled, supervised virtual setting — though VR walking still involves residual physical and cybersickness risks.

Source: VR prototyping framing

What This Changed

Fear-related situation → Explorable spatial task

VR should not simulate danger for stimulation; it can present spatial challenges in a supervised, controllable setting.

Design Translation

Spatial challenge must be felt through the body, not only seen as a VR scene.

Exploratory Prototype Structure

Four Graded Spatial Challenge Types

The undergraduate prototype explored four graded spatial challenge types, from stable ground to reduced visual certainty.

This was a design structure of the undergraduate prototype — not a validated mechanism and not the proposed Master’s experimental design.

01Surface

Challenge

Stable ground and basic gaze targets

User action

Look, step and confirm on flat ground

Why it becomes harder

Baseline level — the simplest spatial condition

02Vertical

Challenge

Height variation with step confirmation

User action

Step onto raised edges after active confirmation

Why it becomes harder

Introduces perceived height risk in a controlled way

03Dynamic

Challenge

Moving pedestrians and attention division

User action

Walk and stay oriented while the environment moves

Why it becomes harder

Adds timing and unpredictability

04Perceptual

Challenge

Low light, depth cues and spatial judgment

User action

Move with reduced visual certainty

Why it becomes harder

Reduces sensory information, demanding spatial judgment

Interaction Design

Action Before Interpretation

The body commits before the action executes. Each interaction turns intention into concrete physical actions with immediate feedback.

Gaze Lock + Trigger Hold

User gazes at the target ring and holds the trigger to confirm readiness before stepping. Reduces anxiety from sudden movement.

Completion Feedback

Every completed task shows explicit confirmation. Bodily action becomes perceived progress.

Evaluation Framework

How the Pilot Session Was Evaluated

The pilot session was designed as a research instrument, not only a demo. Evidence was collected before, during and after training, within one supervised workflow.

Objective Measures

Task completion time and completion status recorded automatically per task.

Subjective Measures

Self-reported confidence collected alongside the objective task records.

Interview

Semi-structured conversation with the participant before and after the session.

Observation

Researcher notes on hesitation, guidance needs and behaviour.

Behavior Log

Interaction events captured automatically during training.

Confidence Rating

Five-point self-report repeated immediately after training.

Raw interaction logs (completion, gaze/trigger events, timestamps) are raw records — not automatically valid psychological outcomes.

Multiple forms of evidence were collected within a single supervised training session. This framework belongs to the undergraduate prototype; the proposed Master’s study uses a separate measurement hierarchy.

Results

What the Pilot Recorded

One supervised pilot session produced a complete before-and-after record — subjective, behavioural and qualitative.

→
→

Behavioural Evidence

Task-level completion time was automatically captured for each training module.

The pilot focused on three selected tasks rather than evaluating the complete four-module framework.

Participant Reflection

  • —She described avoiding complex road surfaces and stairs in daily life.
  • —Fear of falling had reduced her willingness to go out alone.
  • —She mentioned feelings of loneliness when staying at home by herself.
  • —She expressed willingness to try VR-based movement training.

Findings

Finding 01

The participant selected Low before the session and Moderate immediately after the session.

Finding 02

The system successfully captured objective behavioural data throughout all training tasks.

Finding 03

The pilot demonstrated the feasibility of integrating behavioural logging, confidence assessment, and qualitative interviews within a single VR training workflow.

Returning to the Research Question

Can a VR prototype be used to explore relationships among spatial challenge, action, and task-level confidence in older adults with Fear of Falling?

This pilot recorded one participant, three completed tasks with task times, and an immediate Low → Moderate self-report — interaction and feasibility evidence. It is not proof of improvement, not clinical evidence, and not evidence of long-term transfer.

Reflection

What I Learned

  • —Designing the evaluation instrument mattered as much as designing the training itself.
  • —Small, confirmed actions gave the participant a visible record of completion.
  • —A single supervised pilot can test feasibility, not effectiveness — honest framing keeps the evidence useful.
Current Limitations

What This Pilot Cannot Show

Single pilot participant

One supervised session with one older adult participant.

No controlled study

No control group or randomised comparison.

Immediate confidence only

Confidence was measured right after training, not over time.

Efficacy not established

The pilot cannot establish efficacy, causality, construct validity, or real-world transfer.

What I Now Propose

From Prototype to Proposed Study

The undergraduate prototype explored multiple spatial challenge types. The proposed Master's study deliberately narrows the design space to one primary variable: virtual walkway width.

Current proposed study

  • —community-dwelling adults aged 65+
  • —moderate concerns about falling
  • —one primary spatial variable: virtual walkway width
  • —wide / medium / narrow walkway widths
  • —user-informed calibration
  • —counterbalanced within-subject comparison
  • —supervised immersive VR sessions
  • —feasibility focus

Planned measurement hierarchy

01Screening / baseline

Short FES-I · mGES

02Exploratory task-level subjective measures

task-specific walking self-efficacy · perceived safety · perceived challenge

03Behavioural / feasibility measures

HMD-based forward-movement onset latency (candidate behavioural proxy, subject to validation) · traversal time · stop/abort · assistance · data completeness

04Raw interaction logs

completion · gaze/trigger events · timestamps — raw records, not automatically valid psychological outcomes

The undergraduate prototype’s logging is not this measurement hierarchy. This study is proposed and has not yet been conducted.
